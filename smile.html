Research Program

SMILE 🧩

Systems Modeling in Intelligent Learning Environment

SMILE is a parallel research program comprising three GenAI-supported environments for learning about complex systems. Each project takes on a different phenomenon — emergent collective behavior, climate and policy, and epidemic spread — and each explores a different facet of what generative AI can contribute to learning: explanation, reflection, feedback, and critique.

Across all three, the aim is the same: help learners reason about systems as connected wholes — agents, interactions, relations, and causality — rather than as collections of isolated facts, and give learners genuine agency over how they explore and how they ask for help.

Flocking Simulator 🐑

Emergence & collective behavior

An immersive open-world environment where learners control a virtual dog to explore sheep flocks, duck flocks, and bat swarms, supported by the LLM-based Butterfly Tutor grounded in the PAIR-C framework.

Overarching Goals

  • Make complex systems learnable

    Support reasoning about patterns, agents, interactions, relations, and causality — the conceptual moves that factual instruction tends to leave out.

  • Give learners real agency

    Let learners decide what to manipulate, what to ask, whose perspective to take, and when to seek help, rather than moving them through a fixed sequence.

  • Use GenAI as a thinking partner

    Position LLM-based agents as explainers, reflection partners, and — where useful — as objects of critique, not simply as answer machines.

  • Support self-regulated learning

    Build in prediction, confidence monitoring, and structured reflection so learners can observe how their own understanding changes.

  • Ground design in theory and evidence

    Draw on frameworks such as PAIR-C and evaluate each environment with pre/post measures alongside fine-grained interaction log data.

Overarching Research Questions

  1. How can GenAI-supported simulation environments be designed to foster conceptual understanding of complex systems across different domains?
  2. How do different forms of learner agency — parameter control, questioning, perspective-taking, and self-pacing — shape engagement and learning outcomes?
  3. What roles can LLM-based conversational agents play in these environments, and how does each role relate to learning, reflection, and self-regulation?
  4. What do interaction data reveal about how learners engage with open-ended, AI-supported learning environments?
