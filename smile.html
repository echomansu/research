Research Program

SMILE 🧩

Systems Modeling in Intelligent Learning Environment

SMILE is a parallel research program comprising three AI-supported environments for learning about complex systems. Each environment takes on a different phenomenon: animal flocking, climate and policy, and epidemic spread, and each explores a different facet of what generative AI can contribute to learning: supporting multi-dimensional agency, improving informed decision-making, and enabling human-AI co-construction.

Flocking Simulator 🐑

animal flocking & collective behavior

An immersive open-world environment where learners control a virtual dog to explore sheep flocks, duck flocks, and bat swarms, supported by the LLM-based Butterfly Tutor grounded in the PAIR-C framework.

Overarching Goals

  • Make complex systems learnable

    Support learners in reasoning about interactions, feedback loops, causality, and emergence through interactive simulation.

  • Support productive learner agency

    Give learners meaningful choices over how they explore the simulation, what support they seek from AI agent, and which roles or perspectives they adopt, while helping them use these choices strategically for learning.

  • Strengthen informed decision-making capacity

    Help learners anticipate how a system may respond, test and revise alternative strategies, and develop the volitional strength to enact well-reasoned decisions when difficulties arise.

  • Explore complementary roles for GenAI

    Design AI as an inquiry partner or a teachable peer to support human-AI collaboration and co-construction.

  • Develop learner-centered, theory- and evidence-based environments

    Engage learners and stakeholders through co-design, apply frameworks such as PAIR-C, and use learning outcomes and interaction data to iteratively refine each environment.

Overarching Research Questions

  1. How can GenAI-supported simulation environments be designed to foster conceptual understanding of complex systems across different domains?
  2. How do different forms of learner agency — parameter control, questioning, perspective-taking, and self-pacing — shape engagement and learning outcomes?
  3. What roles can LLM-based conversational agents play in these environments, and how does each role relate to learning, reflection, and self-regulation?
  4. What do interaction data reveal about how learners engage with open-ended, AI-supported learning environments?
