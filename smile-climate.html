Climate Change Simulator 🌍

Take a role. Weigh the trade-offs. Watch the planet answer back.

The Climate Change Simulator is a role-based 3D learning environment in which learners step into the shoes of a Citizen or a Farmer, explore climate-related topics in an interactive world, and spend a limited budget of Power Coins on climate policies that visibly change the state of the simulated environment. Throughout the experience, an AI conversational agent named Bobby explains why a policy helped or hurt, interprets extreme events as they appear, and helps learners reflect on how their own thinking changed.

The design goal is not to teach climate facts in isolation, but to make climate change learnable as a system — where energy, agriculture, urbanization, food systems and extreme events are connected, and where policy decisions involve genuine trade-offs rather than obviously right answers.

Research Questions

  1. Design. How might we design a role-based climate simulator integrated with an AI conversational agent to support learners' understanding of climate systems and their reasoning about trade-offs in climate policy decision-making?
  2. Learning & affect. How does interaction with the role-based climate simulator and the AI conversational agent influence learners' climate knowledge, climate anxiety, and willingness to act?
  3. Perception. How do learners perceive the usability and usefulness of the role-based climate simulator and the AI conversational agent?

The Learner Journey

A guided path from role selection through decision-making to reflection.

  1. Choose a stakeholder role

    Learners enter an anonymous ID and pick Citizen or Farmer. The role determines which climate topics they encounter and from whose perspective the trade-offs are felt.

  2. Set a baseline

    Short self-assessments (perceived knowledge, willingness to invest effort, personal importance of climate change) plus one open-ended, role-specific climate question that will be revisited at the end.

  3. Explore a 3D world

    A split-screen layout keeps a city or farm environment visible at all times while a side panel steps through content items — each pairing a short explanation with a 3D model of a concrete climate-related situation.

  4. Decide under constraint

    At decision points, learners spend from a limited budget of Power Coins on climate policies. Costs are revealed only after a choice is made, and unaffordable policies are locked — so learners must reason rather than buy the most expensive option.

  5. Watch the world respond

    Earth Health summarizes the environmental state of the simulation. As it falls, the 3D world visibly degrades and extreme events appear — urban or forest fire, flooding, hurricanes, rising sea level — making consequences immediate rather than abstract.

  6. Ask Bobby, on demand

    The AI agent is always available but never mandatory. Learners open or close the chat as they wish; Bobby proactively offers help right after a policy choice.

  7. Reflect and compare

    Learners answer the opening question a second time and receive AI-generated feedback that explicitly compares their earlier and later answers and names what improved in their reasoning.

  8. Review the outcome

    A results screen shows the chosen policies, remaining Power Coins, and final Earth Health, with a Bobby-generated summary of the positive and negative impacts of each decision — and room to keep asking questions.

  9. Challenge the AI (optional mode)

    In a separate misconception correction mode, the AI deliberately produces an answer containing two subtle climate misconceptions. The learner's job is to find and correct them, one at a time, with the AI judging whether each correction is conceptually valid.

Core Design Features

Ten design requirements were derived from prior work on climate games, simulation-based learning, role-based learning, systems thinking, and AI-supported learning. They translate into six core features.

🎭 Stakeholder roles & content structure

Climate change is split into connected categories rather than one abstract topic: Energy & Emissions, Extreme Events, Urbanization, Agriculture, and Food Systems — each with short explanations and 3D models. Citizen and Farmer share two categories and differ in the rest.

⚖️ Policy decision system

Every policy carries a Power Coin cost and an Earth Health effect. Because the budget is finite and some cheap policies do harm, learners experience climate policy as a balancing process rather than a list of correct answers.

🌪️ Extreme events

Floods, wildfires, hurricanes and rising sea levels appear both as content and as visual consequences in the 3D world, tying long-term emissions to immediate, visible risk — a category under-represented in earlier climate games.

🤖 The AI agent "Bobby"

Bobby answers questions, explains why a policy helped or hurt, interprets the current extreme event, and summarizes the run. Prompts define his role, tone and output format, and always carry the current simulation context.

🔁 Reflection & results

Self-assessments and an open-ended question bracket the simulation, so learners can see their own reasoning change. The results screen connects individual decisions to the final environmental outcome.

🔍 Misconception correction mode

A separate activity that flips the relationship with AI: instead of trusting the agent's answer, learners must critically evaluate and repair it. Two valid corrections complete the task.

Designing Bobby: Context-Aware AI Support

Bobby is never called with the learner's question alone. Each request carries the state the learner is currently looking at, and each prompt specifies the agent's role, the expected tone, and a required output format. Five distinct prompt roles are used across the experience:

  • Adaptive content ordering. Before the simulation, the model orders content categories by relevance given the learner's role, self-rated knowledge, willingness to invest effort, and perceived importance of climate change.
  • In-simulation dialogue. Short, friendly, Markdown-formatted explanations grounded in the content item currently on screen.
  • Extreme-event explanation. Bobby explains why a given event is occurring — while being explicitly forbidden from exposing or justifying the underlying numeric thresholds, which are gameplay values rather than science.
  • Post-assessment feedback. A deliberately generous rubric that rewards conceptual improvement, names exactly how the learner's thinking evolved between their two answers, and avoids penalizing missing jargon.
  • Results summary. A structured review of chosen policies, their positive and negative impacts, and next steps, ending with an invitation to keep asking.

Learner agency is a design constraint throughout: the chat can be opened or closed at will, and no path through the simulation requires interacting with the AI.

Evaluation

A remote user study was conducted with 25 participants recruited through Prolific. Participants completed a Qualtrics pretest, worked through the simulator on their own devices — most completing runs in both stakeholder roles plus the misconception correction mode — and then completed a Qualtrics posttest. Anonymous Prolific IDs linked survey responses to simulator log data.

  • Climate knowledge14 true/false items plus one open-ended systems-thinking question scored with a Components–Mechanisms–Phenomena rubric, and five confidence ratings.
  • Climate anxiety & worryItems adapted from the Climate Change Anxiety Scale and the Climate Change Worry Scale.
  • Willingness to actTen paired pre/post items on climate-friendly behavior and intention.
  • Usability & usefulnessPost-only ratings of task demands, simulation usability, and AI agent usability and usefulness.
  • Interaction logsPolicy choices, Earth Health and Power Coin trajectories, chat messages, and per-section timing.

Systems thinking improved; isolated facts did not. Open-ended explanations of climate change as an interconnected system improved significantly, while true/false knowledge barely moved — unsurprising given a high pretest ceiling. The simulator was better at helping learners connect causes, consequences and decisions than at changing basic factual answers.

Reflection answers improved substantially. AI-scored answers to the stakeholder-specific question rose from 5.71 to 8.71 out of 10 between the pre- and post-simulation attempt.

Concern and agency rose together. Climate worry increased significantly — the simulation made consequences more visible — but so did willingness to act, suggesting concern that is connected to decisions and feedback rather than to helplessness.

Demanding but manageable. Participants rated the simulation as mentally demanding (M = 4.00) and effortful (M = 4.44), yet also reported high perceived success (M = 4.16), low frustration (M = 2.44), and strong overall usability (M = 4.30), with the highest agreement on wanting to use it again.

Learners engaged with the trade-offs. Across 49 simulator runs, 276 policy decisions were logged; 87.7% had a positive Earth Health effect. In their second run participants spent significantly more Power Coins (31.7 → 38.4) without achieving better Earth Health — a concrete demonstration that climate policy is about choosing effective actions, not simply more of them.

The AI agent was used, and valued. Participants wrote on average 7.2 messages per run. The strongest ratings were for helping them explain why policies improved or harmed Earth Health (M = 4.72) and understand the environmental consequences of their decisions (M = 4.68).

Design Implications

  1. Target systems thinking, not factual recall. Ask learners to connect causes, consequences, stakeholders and decisions — that is where the measurable gains appeared.
  2. Make trade-offs visible. Constrain resources so that "more action" and "better outcome" come apart.
  3. Use roles to make decisions concrete. A citizen and a farmer feel the same policy differently, which surfaces climate change as a social and political issue, not only a scientific one.
  4. Pair visual indicators with explanation. Earth Health and Power Coins tell learners that something changed; the AI agent tells them why.
  5. Use AI agents for reflection, not only Q&A. Summarizing decisions and comparing a learner's earlier and later answers made the agent a reflection partner.
  6. Keep AI support context-aware. The agent was most useful when it could refer to the current topic, chosen policy, role, or final result.
  7. Complexity is acceptable if it is structured. A demanding simulation still felt usable when the flow was stepwise and the feedback was clear.

How It Is Built

  • BackendLaravel (PHP) — routing, controllers, session handling, migrations and seeders for stakeholders, content items, policies and the question pool, plus interaction logging.
  • FrontendReact with TypeScript — one page per step of the learner journey, with typed models for sessions, content items, policies and chat messages.
  • 3D environmentThree.js via React Three Fiber and drei, with GLB models preloaded and cached before the simulation begins; the scene reacts to Earth Health through lighting, atmosphere and extreme-event effects.
  • AI layerOpenAI API (GPT-4.1 mini) called exclusively server-side through a single controller, so API keys never reach the browser and every prompt carries controlled context.
  • IterationA pilot study at the LaLaLab reshaped the design: a remaining-decisions counter, hidden policy costs before selection, a lower starting budget, an earlier first extreme event, an explanation for locked policies, and Enter-to-send in the chat.
A note on the model. The simulator is a learning environment, not a climate model. Earth Health, Power Coin costs and policy effects are symbolic gameplay values designed to make trade-offs legible; they are not calibrated economic costs or measured environmental impacts, and outcomes should not be read as climate predictions.
